# school-aide — Problem Statement & Solution

## The Problem

Every day, teachers deliver verbal instructions in a classroom. For most students, following along is natural — they hear the teacher, hold the steps in mind, and get to work. For students with learning disabilities, this chain breaks down at almost every link.

### Who Is Affected

Learning disabilities affect an estimated **1 in 5 students** worldwide. The most common profiles in a typical classroom include:

| Condition | Core challenge in a classroom setting |
|---|---|
| **ADHD** | Difficulty sustaining attention; key steps are missed or forgotten before they can be written down |
| **Dyslexia** | Reading and writing are slow and effortful; copying instructions from a board is a task in itself |
| **Autism Spectrum** | Verbal language can be ambiguous and implicit; figurative or contextual instructions cause confusion |
| **Cognitive / intellectual delays** | Complex multi-step instructions exceed working memory capacity; abstract language is hard to decode |

These students are not failing to learn — they are failing to receive the instruction in a form they can process.

### The Gap

A classroom aide or resource teacher is often present to support these students. But aides face a structural problem: the teacher speaks to the whole class, covering multiple assignments with implicit context, assumed prior knowledge, and natural-language phrasing that is dense with meaning. The aide must then:

1. Mentally parse which assignments were described
2. Translate the language into simpler terms the student can understand
3. Break each assignment into individual, ordered steps
4. Repeat this for every student and every subject, every day

This is cognitively demanding, time-consuming, and inconsistent — the quality of the student's written plan depends entirely on the aide's capacity in that moment.

## Why Verbal Instructions Fail Students with Learning Disabilities

### Working memory overload

Verbal instructions are transient. By the time a teacher finishes saying "for the reading assignment, I want you to read pages 14 to 18, answer the comprehension questions at the bottom, and then write two sentences about what you think the main character should have done differently," a student with ADHD or cognitive delays may have retained only the first fragment — or none of it.

### Ambiguous language

Phrases like "work through the problems," "respond thoughtfully," or "take your time with this" carry implicit meaning that most students infer from context. Students on the autism spectrum or with language processing difficulties may interpret these literally or not at all.

### No external structure

Without a written, step-by-step plan the student can look at repeatedly, the entire cognitive load of remembering what to do next falls on the student's mind — exactly where the disability creates the most friction.

### Anxiety and task-avoidance

When a student cannot parse what they are supposed to do, the result is not confusion — it is avoidance. The student disengages, appears non-compliant, or becomes distressed. The underlying cause (failure to receive a usable instruction) is invisible.

## The Solution: school-aide

school-aide captures the teacher's verbal class instructions as audio, then uses AI to transform them into a structured, plain-language checklist that any student can work through independently.

### How It Helps

**1. Eliminates working memory as a bottleneck**

The student does not need to hold anything in their head. Every step is written down, visible, and persistent. They look at the screen, do the first step, tap it done, and look at the next step. The list does the remembering for them.

**2. Reduces language complexity automatically**

The AI rewrites every instruction at a grade 3–4 reading level: short sentences (maximum 10 words), active verbs, no idioms, no passive voice. "Respond thoughtfully to the comprehension questions" becomes "Step 1: Read each question. Step 2: Write one sentence to answer it."

**3. Provides guided sub-steps for each action**

Each task includes a brief description that tells the student exactly how to start — what to pick up, where to look, what to do first. This removes the ambiguity that causes task-avoidance before the student even begins.

**4. Creates a visual progress structure**

Checkboxes give students a clear sense of progress and completion. For students with ADHD, the act of tapping a task done provides a moment of positive feedback that sustains engagement with the next step. The checklist also makes it obvious when work is finished — removing another common source of anxiety.

**5. Supports the aide without replacing them**

The aide uses the app, reviews the generated list before handing it to the student, and stays present for support. school-aide removes the burden of real-time translation and transcription so the aide can focus on the student's emotional state and understanding rather than note-taking.

**6. Closes the loop with teacher grading**

When the student completes their work, the aide taps **Turn In Work**. The teacher sees only the completed tasks and marks each one right or wrong. This creates a simple, low-friction feedback loop that respects the student's effort and gives the teacher a clear picture of what was understood.

## The Outcome

| Before school-aide | After school-aide |
|---|---|
| Verbal instructions, no written record | Structured checklist generated automatically |
| Complex language requiring interpretation | Grade 3–4 plain language, every time |
| Student must hold steps in working memory | Steps visible on screen at all times |
| Aide spends time transcribing and translating | Aide focuses on the student, not the notes |
| Unclear when work is complete | Student taps each step done; progress is visible |
| No structured feedback mechanism | Teacher grades completed tasks in one screen |

school-aide does not change what is taught. It changes how the instruction reaches the student — turning a spoken, ephemeral, complex message into a written, persistent, simple one. For a student with a learning disability, that difference is everything.
